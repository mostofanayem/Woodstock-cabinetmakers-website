# Brand Voice — Woodstock Cabinet Makers

## Tone

Established, premium, design-led — the voice of a business with 40 years of real work behind it, not a startup trying to sound premium. Confident without being cold: Jack (the owner) comes through in reviews as approachable, a good listener, hands-on — the voice should carry that warmth inside the premium register, not sacrifice one for the other. Think "master craftsman who also happens to have great taste," not a faceless luxury-brand copywriter.

This is a deliberate contrast to Bespoke Woodcraft's plain, down-to-earth voice. Both are honest — Woodstock has genuinely earned premium/luxury language through 40 years of work; using it here isn't overclaiming the way it would be for a pre-revenue brand.

## Vocabulary

**Lean on:**
- Design-forward, premium language: bespoke, custom, considered, timeless, distinctive, personalised, investment, crafted, refined.
- Specific process/design language that shows the "why": "form follows function," "designed around how you actually live," "built to fit exactly how your family uses the space."
- Personal, first-person-adjacent language that keeps Jack visible: "we listen first," "Jack and the team," "we ask what your space needs to do for you" — avoid making it sound like a faceless large firm.

**Avoid:**
- Empty superlatives with nothing behind them: "amazing," "incredible," "best in Brisbane" — fine occasionally if immediately backed by a specific detail, never as a standalone claim.
- Corporate distancing language: "solutions," "leverage," "stakeholders," "best-in-class."
- Cheap/discount framing: never compete on "affordable" or "budget" — that undercuts 40 years of premium positioning.
- Pretending there's no gap: don't write as if the business has been actively posting all along when it hasn't — own the "we're back and busier than ever" angle instead of ignoring the silence.

## Do / Don't patterns

**Do:** Pair premium language with a concrete detail — a real project, a real material, a real client outcome. ("Forty years of custom joinery taught us a kitchen only works if it's built around exactly how you live in it.")
**Don't:** Use premium adjectives as filler with no specifics behind them ("Experience unparalleled excellence in custom cabinetry.")

**Do:** Show finished, styled photography (matches the brand's existing high-end visual identity) alongside real process and behind-the-scenes content that proves the business is active today.
**Don't:** Rely only on polished after-shots — this persona also wants to see the precision and care that goes into fit and finish, especially given past feedback about matching/fit issues.

**Do:** Let Jack's personal, approachable reputation show through — direct quotes, his own words, his presence in photos/video where possible.
**Don't:** Write in a way that sounds like an agency or a large firm with no visible person behind the work.

**Do:** Reference the 40 years and real reviews confidently — this is earned, not a claim to be modest about.
**Don't:** Reference tenure vaguely ("years of experience") without specifics — use the real number (40+ years) and real detail where possible.

## Example phrasing

- "Quality is always a good investment. We've been proving that in Brisbane homes for over 40 years."
- "This wardrobe took three weeks to get exactly right. That's the kind of patience four decades in the trade buys you."
- "Jack still measures every job himself. After 40 years, some habits don't change."
- "We don't renovate spaces. We transform them — and we've been doing it since before flat-pack kitchens existed."
- Avoid: "Experience unparalleled craftsmanship with our world-class custom cabinetry solutions."

## Quick voice check

Before publishing, ask: does this sound like a master tradesman with 40 real years and real finished projects behind him — or does it sound like a marketing agency wrote premium-sounding copy with nothing real behind it? If it's the second one, add a specific detail until it isn't.
